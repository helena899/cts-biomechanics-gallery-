Right-hand grip force across wrist postures

How does force capacity and short-term force maintenance vary across instructed wrist positions in cyclists and sedentary participants?

Exploratory laboratory study · right hand · Kinvent grip curves and Kinovea angle summaries · anonymised

All 17 participants were retained in the quality-control count (8 cyclists, 9 sedentary). P06 has two neutral grip trials only; seven of the 153 planned peak measurements are missing. Time-resolved force curves are available for 16 participants (7 cyclists, 9 sedentary; 144 right-hand curves). Valid angle summaries are available for 13 (4 cyclists, 9 sedentary). Only 12 have both complete force and usable angle summaries.

01 / 04

Figure 1 · Peak grip was lower with bent wrists in 15 of 16 participants

Within-person comparison across instructed neutral, flexion and extension

Median change from neutral: -25.9% in flexion (95% bootstrap CI: −45.5 to −20.8 percentage points; Holm-adjusted p = 0.0013) and -30.9% in extension (95% bootstrap CI: −48.1 to −23.8 percentage points; Holm-adjusted p = 0.00061). 15 of 16 participants had lower mean peak grip in both bent-wrist conditions. The fixed test order prevents a causal attribution to wrist angle alone.

02 / 04

Figure 2 · Neutral grips retained more of their peak force

Force as a fraction of trial maximum (unitless) against relative trial duration (%)

The median force profiles differ descriptively between conditions. No hypothesis test is attached to these curves: the graph illustrates when force is produced and sustained, not a validated fatigue or CTS outcome.

03 / 04

Figure 3 · Cyclists and sedentary participants showed overlapping force retention

Exploratory group comparison: cyclists (n = 7) and sedentary participants (n = 9)

For flexion, cyclists had a median retention ratio of 81.4% (IQR 79.1–94.3%) versus 84.1% (IQR 78.8–93.5%) in sedentary participants (exploratory Mann–Whitney p = 0.681, unadjusted). Individual distributions overlap; this is not evidence of a difference in CTS susceptibility.

04 / 04

Figure 4 · Measured wrist angles varied within each instructed posture

Quality-control context, not evidence of a cyclist–sedentary difference

Usable Kinovea-derived summaries exist for 13 of 17 participants (4 cyclists and 9 sedentary). Wrist angles vary substantially even within the same instructed position. Because no goniometer-fixed target angle was used and the tracking/annotation quality differs, the plot illustrates a measurement limitation rather than a causal or clinical group effect.

What can we conclude?

  • Most defensible result: grip force is lower in the bent-wrist laboratory conditions than in the neutral reference for most participants. This is a posture-associated observation, not proof of causation because trial order was fixed.
  • Group comparison: cyclists and sedentary participants overlap substantially. A small non-significant difference does not prove equivalence.
  • Additional signal: short-term force profiles can be studied without synchronising the video; the retention ratio is an exploratory descriptive metric, not a diagnostic fatigue score.
  • CTS relevance: published studies link wrist posture and force to carpal-tunnel pressure and occupational exposures to incident CTS. Here, actual cycling force, contact pressure, exposure duration during riding, symptoms, and incident CTS were not measured.

What I would change in the protocol

Randomise the three wrist postures; include practice trials and longer rest; measure/verify wrist angle for each grip; record quantitative force and angles synchronously; measure actual handlebar loading and median-nerve symptoms for research explicitly aimed at CTS risk.
